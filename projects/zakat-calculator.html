Human Appeal Canada — Zakat Calculator Redesign

Using web analytics, behavioural research, UX design and AI-assisted development to transform a complex financial calculation into a more transparent, interactive digital experience.

Google Analytics 4 Hotjar / Contentsquare Data analysis UX research Heatmap analysis Figma Replit AI AI-assisted development Functional prototyping GitHub HTML / CSS / JavaScript

Overview

Human Appeal Canada's Zakat Calculator helps donors estimate their annual Zakat obligation using eligible financial assets, liabilities and Nisab thresholds.

This project explored how a more data-informed, human-centred approach could improve the existing calculator. The objective was to simplify the process, make financial inputs easier to understand and provide greater visibility into how Zakat is calculated.

I combined Google Analytics 4 acquisition data with Hotjar/Contentsquare behavioural analytics to investigate how users reached and interacted with the calculator. These findings, alongside a UX review, helped shape the redesign requirements.

I then created low-fidelity wireframes in Figma and used Replit's AI-assisted development capabilities to translate the designs and functional specifications into an interactive application.

The application was subsequently organized into separate source files and published as an interactive prototype through GitHub Pages, with its source code available for review.

01 / Defining the problem

Calculating Zakat requires users to consider multiple financial categories, eligible liabilities and calculation rules. The experience should make that complexity manageable without hiding the logic behind the result.

A central concern with the existing experience was the number of steps involved in reaching an estimate. When users must enter financial values and then trigger a separate calculation, they have limited visibility into how their inputs affect the result throughout the process.

This creates the potential for a black-box experience: the user supplies information but does not continuously see how the calculated amount is determined.

The redesign focused on three interconnected objectives:

  1. Reduce friction: Minimize unnecessary interactions and make financial categories more discoverable.
  2. Improve transparency: Show a live estimate and explain how entered values contribute to Zakat owed.
  3. Support informed giving: Clarify the calculation before users proceed to a donation.

These objectives represented design hypotheses to investigate and test, rather than demonstrated improvements in user completion or conversions.

02 / Analyzing the existing user journey

I used two complementary analytics platforms to investigate the existing Zakat Calculator experience.

Google Analytics 4 provided acquisition and traffic information, while Hotjar/Contentsquare provided behavioural measures and interaction data from the calculator page.

Rather than treating the metrics as isolated observations, I used them to examine three questions:

  1. How are visitors reaching the calculator?
  2. How are users interacting with the existing experience?
  3. Which parts of the interaction and calculation process should be prioritized for redesign?

Analytics sources: Google Analytics 4, Pages and Screens export (January 1–October 29, 2026); Hotjar/Contentsquare engagement dashboard (January 1–March 31, 2026); and Hotjar desktop click heatmap exported November 5, 2026, covering the preceding 30 days. Metrics are kept within their original reporting scopes.

Finding 01 / Campaign-driven traffic

GA4 showed that 75.2% of recorded calculator page views originated from Google and Bing paid search. Together, these sources generated 2,344 of the 3,116 page views in the supplied export.

This established paid search as a significant acquisition channel for the calculator and highlighted the importance of a clear entry experience.

Visitors arriving from campaigns should be able to immediately identify the calculator's purpose, find relevant financial inputs and begin a calculation.

Design implication: Make asset categories visible and reduce unnecessary decisions at the beginning of the journey.

Finding 02 / Engagement and content visibility

The Hotjar/Contentsquare calculator page overview recorded approximately 1,400 sessions and 7,300 clicks, with an average time on page of 1 minute and 30 seconds.

The average scroll depth was 67.9%. This indicated how far users progressed vertically through the page on average, raising questions about the visibility of financial inputs, guidance and donation actions.

Although an average scroll-depth measure cannot establish whether specific elements were seen, it supported investigating whether important information should appear earlier or remain visible throughout the calculation.

Design implication: Prioritize information hierarchy and keep the evolving estimate accessible while users enter financial information.

Finding 03 / Potential interaction friction

Hotjar/Contentsquare recorded 118 sessions with rage clicks and a reported drop-off rate of 72% in the calculator page overview.

Rage clicks are repeated interactions that may indicate confusion, unexpected behaviour or unresponsive controls.

These signals provided reasons to investigate the interaction design more closely, but did not establish the causes of user frustration.

Similarly, the platform's drop-off metric could not be interpreted as a 72% calculation abandonment rate without the underlying event definitions and completion data.

Design implication: Simplify controls, provide clearer explanations and reduce avoidable interactions.

Interpreting the click distribution

The largest identifiable group of interactions involved financial input fields, accounting for 12 of the 27 recorded clicks.

This reinforced the importance of making the calculator's input structure intuitive. Asset categories, field labels and contextual guidance should be easy to understand.

The explicit Calculate action received six recorded clicks, representing 22.2% of the desktop heatmap sample.

This highlighted the existing dependence on a separate calculation action and informed the decision to explore a continuously updating estimate.

The donation link received two recorded clicks, while the Nisab selector received one. These counts highlighted interactions worth examining in a fuller journey analysis.

However, click counts alone cannot determine whether a calculation was completed, whether a user donated, or whether a specific control caused abandonment.

The heatmap sample was also limited to desktop interactions. Its distribution should not be assumed to represent mobile behaviour.

Connecting the findings

Taken together, the analytics highlighted three important aspects of the existing journey.

  1. Acquisition: Paid search was a major source of calculator traffic, emphasizing the importance of a clear entry experience.
  2. Interaction: Behavioural analytics and the desktop heatmap highlighted financial inputs, calculation actions and potential friction as relevant areas of investigation.
  3. Measurement: The available reports did not provide a verified calculation-to-donation funnel, limiting conclusions about completion and conversion.

The central redesign hypothesis was that a more transparent and streamlined experience could reduce unnecessary effort and support user understanding.

The analytics informed which parts of the journey deserved attention, while the UX review and functional requirements guided the specific solutions.

Analytical limitations

The GA4 export, Q1 behavioural dashboard and recent desktop heatmap covered different periods and measurement units.

Page views, sessions and clicks were not treated as interchangeable denominators or combined into an artificial conversion rate.

The available evidence supported redesign priorities and testable hypotheses, but did not establish causal relationships or post-redesign performance improvements.

03 / Translating insights into requirements

The analysis and UX review informed a set of functional requirements designed to improve efficiency, calculation transparency, asset discoverability and clarity in giving.

A continuously updating estimate

Rather than requiring users to complete every entry and trigger a separate calculation before viewing a result, the proposed calculator would update the estimated amount as inputs change.

The live summary would communicate listed wealth, relevant liabilities, the amount used for Zakat, the applied 2.5% rate and the selected Nisab threshold.

This requirement was intended to reduce unnecessary interactions and make the calculation process more interpretable.

Clearer financial inputs

  1. Use CAD as the default calculation and reporting currency.
  2. Provide structured account-type dropdowns with an Other option.
  3. Make stocks and cryptocurrency clearly visible as asset categories.
  4. Include a dedicated foreign-currency input area while maintaining CAD-based totals.
  5. Group related financial assets and liabilities into recognizable categories.
  6. Provide contextual explanations and educational video guidance where relevant.

Transparent calculation logic

  1. Clearly explain how eligible wealth is determined.
  2. Display the 2.5% calculation and the basis used to derive the estimated obligation.
  3. Identify the applicable Nisab threshold.
  4. Explore automatic Silver Nisab selection when eligible wealth falls below the Gold threshold.
  5. Simplify the lunar-year question through an assumed eligibility condition in the proposed experience.

Nisab selection and lunar-year assumptions would require subject-matter validation before production use.

Separate interest-related giving

The calculator also needed to distinguish between Zakat owed and interest received that a user intends to set aside for donation.

  1. Track interest received separately from Zakat.
  2. Exclude interest received from the Zakat calculation.
  3. Explain this separation through contextual guidance.
  4. Provide a separate interest-related action when a positive amount is entered.
  5. Display the separate amount in the final giving review.

04 / From data to design decisions

To connect the analysis with the proposed experience, I mapped the key findings and usability requirements to design responses and measurable outcomes.

Acquisition → Immediate clarity

Evidence: 75.2% of page views in the supplied GA4 export originated from Google and Bing paid search.

Design response: Prioritize a clear starting point with visible financial categories and immediate feedback.

Evaluation: Compare calculator start rates across acquisition channels.

Input interactions → Better discoverability

Evidence: Financial input fields accounted for 12 of 27 clicks in the desktop heatmap sample.

Design response: Use recognizable input categories, structured controls and contextual explanations.

Evaluation: Measure input error rates, asset discovery success and task completion.

Calculation action → Real-time feedback

Evidence: The Calculate button received six recorded clicks in the desktop heatmap.

Design response: Explore a live estimate that updates during financial entry, rather than relying entirely on a separate calculation action.

Evaluation: Compare time to estimate, task success and user comprehension.

Giving journey → Measurable progression

Evidence: The desktop heatmap recorded two donation-link clicks, while the historical reports did not establish complete calculation-to-donation progression.

Design response: Make the review and giving transition clearer, with a proposed event-tracking framework for future evaluation.

Evaluation: Track calculation completion, donation initiation and, where attributable, verified donation completion.

The project progressed beyond requirements and UX concepts into an interactive application.

I used Figma to establish the proposed information hierarchy and Replit's AI-assisted development tools to translate those designs into a functional calculator prototype.

Low-fidelity UX design

I developed low-fidelity Figma wireframes exploring the calculator's financial input structure, information organization and calculation feedback.

The wireframes served as visual references for the functional prototype and helped communicate the intended experience during development.

Zakat Calculator — Figma wireframes

AI-assisted development with Replit

I used Replit's AI-assisted development capabilities by providing my Figma wireframes, functional requirements and calculation specifications as inputs to the development process.

Rather than starting with a complete manually coded application, I used structured instructions and iterative refinement to guide the generation of the calculator's interface and application logic.

The process involved four connected activities:

  1. Design translation: Feed the wireframes into the AI-assisted environment to communicate layout, structure and interactions.
  2. Requirements specification: Describe the financial input categories, calculation behaviour, transparency requirements and giving flow.
  3. Iterative development: Review the generated interface and refine the implementation using additional instructions.
  4. Functional prototyping: Develop an interactive demonstration of the proposed calculator experience.

This workflow demonstrated my ability to adopt emerging AI development tools, communicate technical requirements and bridge UX design with application implementation.

The process also highlighted the importance of evaluating AI-generated functionality rather than assuming that generated code is correct.

Functional prototype requirements

The functional prototype was developed around the following requirements and interaction goals.

  1. Dynamic calculation: Support interactive financial entry and updating Zakat estimates.
  2. Asset handling: Provide structured ways to enter savings, investments and other relevant asset categories.
  3. Calculation breakdown: Make the estimated obligation and its calculation basis understandable.
  4. Nisab guidance: Communicate the applicable threshold and support the proposed selection logic.
  5. Separate interest treatment: Distinguish interest-related giving from Zakat owed.
  6. Responsive interaction: Support use across desktop and mobile screen sizes.
  7. Design consistency: Translate the wireframes into an interface visually aligned with Human Appeal Canada.
  8. Review and donation: Present the result clearly and provide a path toward the giving process.

These requirements document the intended functionality of the prototype. They should not be interpreted as a completed independent verification of every calculation scenario.

06 / Measuring the redesigned experience

The available analytics helped identify areas for redesign but did not provide a complete picture of the calculation and giving journey.

To address that gap, I proposed a measurement framework for evaluating how visitors interact with a redesigned calculator.

Proposed calculator conversion funnel

  1. Calculator viewed: A visitor arrives at the calculator page.
  2. Calculation started: The visitor begins entering financial information.
  3. Estimate reviewed: The visitor reaches a valid, reviewable estimate.
  4. Donation initiated: The visitor proceeds toward the donation flow.
  5. Donation completed: A donation is verified and reliably attributed to the calculator journey, where possible.

Proposed measurement model. The available historical reports do not contain complete counts for each funnel stage.

Defining performance indicators

  1. Calculator start rate: Sessions with a calculation start divided by calculator page sessions.
  2. Calculation completion rate: Valid completed estimates divided by started calculations.
  3. Step abandonment rate: The proportion of journeys that do not progress to the next defined funnel stage.
  4. Median time to estimate: Median elapsed time between beginning a calculation and reviewing a valid result.
  5. Donation progression rate: Completed calculations followed by donation initiation divided by completed calculations.
  6. Input error rate: Frequency of defined input validation errors relative to relevant input attempts.

Proposed GA4 event tracking

A more detailed event taxonomy would support analysis of interaction patterns and progression through the calculation experience.

  1. calculator_start — first meaningful calculation interaction.
  2. asset_category_selected — predefined asset category selection.
  3. calculator_help_opened — interaction with explanatory content.
  4. calculation_error_displayed — predefined validation error category.
  5. calculation_review_viewed — valid calculation reaches the review stage.
  6. donation_flow_started — user proceeds toward giving.
  7. donation_completed — verified donation where reliable attribution and consent permit.

Events would require clear triggering definitions, implementation testing and validation before supporting reliable reporting.

Defining redesign success

The proposed evaluation framework connected each UX objective with a measurable outcome.

  1. Efficiency: Reduce the median time required to produce a valid estimate without compromising accuracy.
  2. Completion: Increase the percentage of started calculations reaching a valid review.
  3. Transparency: Improve users' ability to explain how their calculated result was determined.
  4. Input clarity: Reduce validation errors and improve success when identifying relevant asset categories.
  5. Donation progression: Increase the proportion of completed calculations that proceed toward giving.
  6. Measurement quality: Establish reliable event coverage across the calculator journey.

No specific improvement percentages were assigned without an appropriate baseline and validation plan.

Privacy-conscious measurement

Because the calculator handles sensitive financial information, the proposed tracking focuses on interactions rather than entered financial values.

Analytics should not collect individual balances, interest amounts, asset values or personally identifiable information.

Reporting should use approved aggregate information and respect applicable privacy and consent requirements.

07 / Validation and next steps

The functional prototype provides a foundation for exploring the redesigned experience, but further validation would be necessary before production use.

  1. Usability testing: Evaluate whether visitors can locate asset categories and complete realistic calculation tasks.
  2. Calculation validation: Test financial scenarios against independently established expected results.
  3. Subject-matter review: Validate Nisab selection, lunar-year assumptions, foreign currency and eligible asset treatment.
  4. Responsive evaluation: Test desktop and mobile interactions, especially financial inputs and estimate visibility.
  5. Accessibility review: Assess keyboard navigation, screen-reader feedback, form labels, tooltips and validation messages.
  6. Analytics validation: Verify event definitions and instrument the proposed funnel before launch.
  7. Post-implementation analysis: Evaluate completion, time to estimate and donation progression using comparable data.
